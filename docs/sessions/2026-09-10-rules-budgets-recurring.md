## The bug worth reading first

Halfway through Epic 6 I checked whether the migration had widened a CHECK
constraint. It had not — and neither had the one before it.

`Enum(SomeEnum, native_enum=False)` stores a VARCHAR plus a CHECK listing the
allowed values. **Adding a member to the Python enum changes nothing about the
column**, so `alembic revision --autogenerate` emits no operation and
`alembic check` reports a clean tree. The database goes on rejecting the value.

Two enums had drifted:

- `ExpenseSource` gained `RECURRING` in this session.
- `NotificationKind` gained `BUDGET_WARNING` and `BUDGET_EXCEEDED` **in the
  commit before it**. Every budget alert would have failed against a migrated
  database.

Every test passed throughout, because `tests/conftest.py` built the test schema
with `Base.metadata.create_all` — from the models. The tests were checking the
app against a schema that is never deployed. CI would not have caught it either:
it migrates one database and then tests against a different one.

Three changes, in increasing order of importance:

1. Migration `453f7823ac5b` rewrites both CHECK constraints by hand.
2. **The test database is now built by running the migrations.** Slower by a
   couple of seconds, and it means green tests are evidence about the schema
   that actually ships.
3. `tests/api/test_schema_matches_models.py` compares every VARCHAR enum column
   against its constraint in the database. Verified it fails by adding a bogus
   enum member and watching it go red.

Two smaller things fell out of the same look: both autogenerated downgrades used
`op.drop_constraint(None, ...)` for a foreign key, which cannot execute. Named
both. The whole nine-migration chain now runs `base → head → base → head`
cleanly, which nothing had ever checked.

**This is the best story in the project for the report.** Not "we found a bug",
but *what our tests were not testing, and how we found out*.

## 3.4 — Standing split rules

A `WEIGHT` split already let someone type weights on one expense. A rule is the
same arithmetic **agreed once and applied automatically** — the part typing per
expense never covered.

Weights rather than percentages, because percentages are just weights that must
add to 100, and square metres and nights stayed are numbers people already have.

- *Rent by room size* — category `RENT`, weights 14 / 12 / 10
- *Nights stayed* — no category, so it claims everything on the trip

One rule per category plus one catch-all. The catch-all needs its own **partial
unique index**: a `UNIQUE` constraint does not constrain NULLs in Postgres, so
without it a group could hold two.

Precedence in one line: **whoever names participants wins.** A rule only fills
the gap left by not naming them. An expense that says exactly who is on it is
never quietly re-split by a rule somebody set last month, and
`apply_split_rule: false` opts out for one expense.

Two things that had to keep working when people move out, and are the tests I
care most about here:

- A rule whose members have partly left **drops the departed shares and
  reweights the rest** — the remaining rooms are still the sizes they were.
- A rule nobody is left in is **ignored, not fatal**. Falling back to an equal
  split beats refusing to record rent.

Rules apply at creation, never on edit. Recategorising an expense is a
correction, not an instruction to redivide money already recorded.

## 4.5 — Duplicate payments, from both ends

"Did we pay this twice?" turned out to be two questions.

**Stopping it.** `Idempotency-Key` on expense and settlement creation. A phone on
the underground sends rent, the reply never arrives, the app retries — without a
key that is two rent payments. The key row is written **in the same transaction
as the expense**, so a rejected request takes its key down with it and can be
retried after fixing the typo. Reusing a key for a different body is a 409:
that is a client bug, and answering it with the first request's expense would
hide it.

A concurrent duplicate is settled by the unique index rather than by a read:
both requests race to insert, the loser blocks until the winner commits, then
reads the winner's row and replays it. The insert sits in a SAVEPOINT so losing
costs one statement.

**Finding it.** `GET /groups/{id}/analytics/duplicates`. Deliberately *not* the
same question as anomaly detection — that asks whether one expense is unlike its
own history; this asks whether two expenses are the same event, which needs
money, timing and wording to agree at once.

The scoring is blunt on purpose (money 0.5, wording 0.3, timing 0.2) because a
person reads the result, and each pair carries plain sentences saying why. The
**three-day window is the load-bearing parameter**: it is what keeps January rent
from being reported as a duplicate of February rent.

Nothing is merged or deleted. Two coffees at 12.00 on the same day look exactly
like a double tap and are not one.

## 4.6 — Budgets

Per category, per month, plus an optional ceiling on the whole group. Measured on
expense **totals**, not one person's share: a budget is a limit on what leaves
the household. A budget on `OTHER` also counts uncategorised spending, exactly
as analytics does.

Where the lines sit lives in `domain/budgets.py` so it is arguable in one place.
Spending *exactly* the limit is `EXCEEDED`, not `OK` — the next coffee is over
it, and a budget that only complains at 100.01 is one nobody notices in time.

**The part worth reviewing is the alerting.** Alerts fire from inside
`create_expense`, because the moment an expense lands is the only moment anybody
wants to hear about it — but at most **once per budget per month per level**. The
last month and level warned about live on the budget row. Without that the
twelfth expense over the line raises a twelfth notification and everyone stops
reading them.

Changing a budget clears its alert state, so raising one that was already blown
does not leave everyone silently un-warnable for the rest of the month.

## Epic 6 — Recurring bills

**The distinction the whole epic turns on: `amount` may be null.**

- **Rent is 3600 every month.** The amount is known, so the bill posts itself.
- **Electricity is whatever the meter says.** No amount, so the bill *reminds*
  and never invents a number.

A design that only handled fixed amounts would have been quietly useless for
exactly the bills people argue about.

**Nothing runs on a scheduler.** No Celery, no APScheduler — a service to keep
alive for the sake of a `while` loop. The work is idempotent and triggered
instead: the app calls `POST .../recurring-bills/run` on load, and
`backend/run_due_bills.py` exists for a real cron. Running twice in a minute
changes nothing; running a month late posts the month it missed, one expense per
period, because each of those months really did have a rent payment in it.

The date arithmetic earned its own pure module. A bill due on the **31st** must
land on the 28th in February and then go *back* to the 31st in March — so the
anchor day is stored separately from the next due date and re-applied each time.
Using February's clamped 28th as March's anchor would walk the bill backwards
three days a year, permanently.

Two smaller calls:

- "Bi-monthly" became **`EVERY_2_MONTHS`**. Half the world reads bi-monthly as
  twice a month, and Israeli utility bills arrive every two.
- Expenses are attributed to **whoever set the bill up**, not to whoever
  triggered the run. Otherwise opening the app makes you the author of somebody
  else's rent, and the cron job has no author at all.

**The composition I am most pleased with:** a bill that names no participants
lets a standing split rule decide. So *rent, monthly* plus *rent by room size*
gives rent that posts itself on the right proportions, with neither feature
knowing about the other.

`expense_service.build_expense` was split out — everything creating an expense
involves, minus the commit — so a bill can post several months in one
transaction without anything reimplementing splitting, notifications or the
budget check.

## What surprised us

- **`is_worse(level, previously_said)`** turned out to be the whole of the
  alert-once logic, in three lines, once the levels were ranked. The first draft
  was a pile of conditionals about periods and thresholds.
- The duplicate detector's default threshold sits exactly where a pair that is
  weak on *all three* counts (near-but-not-equal amount, half-matching title,
  far edge of the window) scores 0.55 and is dropped. Any two being strong is
  enough. My first test guessed the score wrong; the code was right.
- Postgres `now()` bit again, indirectly: `clock_timestamp()` from the last
  session is what makes several expenses posted in one recurring-bill run land
  in the feed in the order they were posted.
